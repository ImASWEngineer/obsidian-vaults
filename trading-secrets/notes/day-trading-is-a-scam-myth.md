## Summary
This note addresses the common misconception that day trading is inherently a scam. The book argues that while it is extremely difficult and has a high failure rate, it is a legitimate profession based on skill, not a fraudulent scheme.

## Key Arguments
- **High Failure Rate vs. Scam**: The high failure rate (see [[why-most-traders-fail]]) is often mistaken for evidence of a scam. However, many difficult professions have high failure rates (e.g., professional sports, starting a restaurant).
- **Skill vs. Deception**: A scam implies deception. Legitimate day trading is based on analyzing market dynamics and applying a strategy with a statistical edge. See [[luck-vs-skill-in-trading]].
- **The "Gurus" Problem**: The perception of a scam is often fueled by unscrupulous "gurus" selling get-rich-quick schemes. It's crucial to distinguish these from legitimate educators. See [[criteria-for-a-good-trading-teacher]].
- **It's a Zero-Sum Game (Mostly)**: In the short-term, trading can be seen as a zero-sum game where one trader's gain is another's loss. This competitive reality contributes to its difficulty, but does not make it a scam.
